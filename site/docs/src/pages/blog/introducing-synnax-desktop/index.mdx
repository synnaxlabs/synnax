A week ago we launched Synnax Desktop. It's our Core, Console, and device drivers
bundled together, built for the bench, and free for commercial use.

It's also a big change in how we sell Synnax. For years, the only way to get your hands
on it was to talk to us first. Desktop flips that around. In this post, I'll walk you
through what Desktop is, why we think it's awesome, and why we decided to lead with the
product instead of a sales process.

## Two ways to buy software

Nowadays, my job is mostly communication and sales. But before that, I was a laser
focused systems engineer, and I spent a lot of time procuring software: databases, ETL
tools, CI/CD pipelines, managed Kubernetes services, you name it.

It always went one of two ways.

Story 1 goes straight to sales. You submit a contact form, get on the phone with a sales
rep, do a demo ... or two ... or three ... and then spend weeks convincing your boss to
pay for a product you've never actually used. And, often times, the really expensive
tool you buy ends up not being what you expected.

There's a common joke on industrial automation subreddits that goes like this:

Unlike the joke, the second pathway is different. Instead of submitting a form for a
demo, you can download the product yourself. Sometimes the full-fledged version,
sometimes with restricted use. You're able to try it, enjoy using it, find value, and
use practical deployment to show your team that the paid edition is a worthwhile
investment.

If you're an engineer, story 2 is dramatically better than story 1. You directly
interact with the tool, see where it works and where it doesn't, and decide if it's
worth your time and investment.

The companies and tools I respect the most operate this way: Linear, Vercel, and Figma.
Most recently, I signed up for an enterprise subscription to
[Paradev](https://paradev.com). The product was clearly so much better than anything
else out there, that I knew I had to pay for it.

Desktop is our story 2. We're putting the product first, and focusing deeply on the user
experience and technical excellence. It's a risk, but it's one we're happy to take
because it's the way we'd like to evaluate Synnax if we were engineers at our customers'
companies.

## So what is Synnax Desktop?

Synnax Desktop is a free edition of Synnax designed specifically for benchtop testing.
It combines our Core, Console, and device drivers into what we think is one of the best
desktop data acquisition, control, and data review tools on the market.

It's easy to set up. Download and run Desktop, and it'll immediately detect the data
acquisition devices you have connected. The path from first seeing the tool to actually
getting use out of it is as short as possible. Under the hood, we run the key components
of the enterprise edition: device drivers, the Arc runtime, a Core, and the Console, all
in one.

## How does it compare?

You've probably got one of these on your bench already. Here's the short version.

|                        | You build                      | Control and sequences        | Price                                         |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **Synnax Desktop**     | Nothing. Install it and go.    | Schematics and Arc           | Free, including commercial use                |
| **LabVIEW**            | The whole tool                 | Whatever you program         | A quote from NI. Community is non-commercial. |
| **FlexLogger**         | A logging configuration        | Events in Pro, no sequencer  | Lite is free. Professional is paid.           |
| **InfluxDB + Grafana** | The integration and dashboards | None. That's another script. | Free and open source                          |

And here's the long one.

### LabVIEW

LabVIEW is the most common by far. If you're in a lab, there's a good chance you're
either someone who uses a VI written by someone who left the company a while ago, or
you're in charge of maintaining bespoke VIs for different test applications. LabVIEW has
clearly earned its place: decades of reliability improvements, integration with the NI
hardware ecosystem, and awesome tooling like FPGA programming.

But good LabVIEW tools require good LabVIEW programmers. They are hard to come by, busy,
and often times stretched thin in their work. If you want to do it yourself, you have to
build the tool, and the free Community Edition is non-commercial. NI doesn't list prices
for the rest: you request a quote. Story 1 again.

Desktop is that tool, already built. If you need deterministic control on an FPGA, keep
LabVIEW. For everything else on the bench, try Desktop.

### FlexLogger

FlexLogger is NI's no-code data logger, and it's the closest thing to Desktop out there.
If your bench is all NI hardware, it's a great way to get a logging setup running
without writing a single line of LabVIEW. There's a lot to like: it's built by the same
people who make the hardware, it's easy to configure, and the Lite edition is free.

But FlexLogger is really built for NI hardware and for logging. Plenty of benches aren't
all NI, though. There's a LabJack someone bought for a quick test, or a PLC talking
Modbus, and connecting those means a plug-in built in LabVIEW. You can set up events in
the Professional edition, but there's no sequencer, so the moment you want to
pressurize, hold, and vent, you're back to writing LabVIEW or Python.

Desktop talks to all of that hardware at once, and runs your sequences in the same
window you're plotting in.

### InfluxDB and Grafana

InfluxDB and Grafana are what software engineers reach for. If someone on your team came
from the software world, they've probably pitched it to you as the free option. And I
get why: both are open source, Grafana makes beautiful dashboards, and for monitoring
servers it's hard to beat.

But neither tool was built for hardware. Nothing in the stack talks to a DAQ card, so
someone has to write and maintain a script to get data in. Grafana can't control
anything, so opening a valve is another script. Dashboards also refresh every 5 seconds
by default, which is fine for CPU usage but not for watching a pressure spike. By the
time it all works, you've built a custom tool out of five different pieces, and someone
has to own it.

Desktop is the whole stack in one tool, with no bolting together a DAQ, a Python script,
Telegraf, InfluxDB, and Grafana. And the database underneath was built for sensor data:
we [benchmarked it against InfluxDB and Timescale](/blog/one-billion-rows).

## So why didn't we do this sooner?

Honestly? Because story 1 works.

It lets you control the process. Instead of waiting for an engineer to fall in love with
your tool, you can meticulously work through finding an engineering champion, running
demos with the right stakeholders, and getting to procurement earlier.

It also works better for products that take a lot of work to fit into a customer's
infrastructure, and that was early Synnax. We often had to build significant new
features before our tools worked with a customer's hardware. If we'd handed out a free
version as is, most people wouldn't have found a fast enough path to value.

That's no longer true. Synnax now works on a bench straight out of the box: plug in a
LabJack, and you're plotting data in minutes.

And deep down, all of us at Synnax are engineers. We want Synnax to be built by
engineers, for engineers. Instead of being fed a problem and solution pair by a sales
team, you should be able to discern for yourself where our product seriously unlocks
productivity for your team, and where it doesn't.

Synnax Desktop is a renewed, focused commitment to that ethos. We build tools for
hardcore engineers, and we're putting our product center stage as the evidence for how
and why we build.

## When should you talk to us?

Remember the end of story 2? You show your team the paid edition is worth it, and then
you start the sales process. Desktop is designed for every step before that one. And
yes, it's also designed to eventually make you interested in having a conversation with
us about our enterprise edition.

Desktop is for one person on one computer, with up to 50 channels. Synnax Enterprise is
for a team that shares one system. You run a Synnax Core on your own servers and get:

- Access from other computers, including the Console in a web browser.
- Users, roles, and permissions.
- Drivers on other computers, including NI CompactRIOs.
- Linux, Docker, and air-gapped deployments.
- As many channels as your license allows.

The day your bench turns into a test stand, or one engineer turns into five, is the day
we'd love to talk.
